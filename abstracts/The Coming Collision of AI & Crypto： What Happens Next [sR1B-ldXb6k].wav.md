## Abstract

PART 1 — ABSTRACT

This conversation explores the fast-approaching convergence of AI agents and crypto infrastructure, analyzing how these two technologies could jointly reshape digital identity, monetization, content attribution, and trust online. Participants argue that as AI agents proliferate and act on behalf of users across many platforms, persistent universal identities, largely enabled by blockchains, will be critical for both agents and individuals. At stake is the future of internet economics, content creation incentives, and the balance between openness and control as platforms, creators, and bots interact at scale. Tensions remain over how to design interoperable identity and context frameworks, how to equitably compensate creators as AI scrapes and synthesizes information, and how to differentiate trustworthy humans from increasingly capable autonomous agents—raising open questions about privacy, centralization, and whether new decentralized mechanisms can develop quickly enough to preserve a flourishing, open internet.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- Universal identity for AI agents is essential for them to operate consistently across different platforms and contexts; blockchains offer a portable, permissionless solution, reducing dependence on extractive centralized platforms.
- Persistent, interoperable digital context (personal preferences, identity, history) is necessary for AI agents to function usefully, and only blockchain tech appears to provide forward-compatible, user-owned management of that context.
- Proof of personhood systems, such as world ID, are becoming critical in distinguishing bots from humans online, both to protect user trust and to preserve resources in a world where bots vastly outnumber humans.
- The traditional web monetization model—based on directing human traffic to content providers in exchange for ad revenue—is breaking down as AI directly consumes and synthesizes content, threatening open web economics.
- AI web crawlers are now a dominant form of internet traffic, accelerating content restrictions and paywalls as creators push back against uncompensated data extraction; this creates a scarcity of high-quality data for models.
- Crypto-based micropayments and automated attribution can enable new models where agents or bots pay for access to content or APIs in real time, potentially restoring incentives for creators and establishing automated, machine-scale marketplaces.
- The risk of internet “data silos” increases as large AI firms cut exclusive content deals, threatening the openness and long-tail creator inclusivity that characterized the early web.
- Decentralized, privacy-preserving identity and payment frameworks (including zero-knowledge proofs) could allow users to selectively prove attributes or receive payment for attention/data without typical surveillance by centralized ad platforms.
- Institutional inertia and technical friction—in getting content platforms, creators, and users to adopt new standards—remain significant barriers, as does the need to ensure fair attribution and non-exploitative platform dynamics.
- There are open questions about enforcement: namely, how to reliably distinguish between human and bot traffic at scale, and how to manage the incentives of various actors while maintaining an open, equitable internet ecosystem.
