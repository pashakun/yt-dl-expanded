PART 1 — ABSTRACT

This conversation centers on the rapid evolution and growing institutionalization of stablecoins in African and other emerging markets, as seen through the operations of Yellowcard, a leading African crypto exchange. Emphasizing a shift from consumer speculation to practical business and B2B payment use cases, the discussion highlights how stablecoins are filling gaps left by traditional banking infrastructure—especially for cross-border transactions where access to US dollars is otherwise difficult or expensive. The rise of stablecoins matters because it is rewiring international payment rails, integrating emerging market businesses into global commerce, and reshaping how value moves across borders, potentially disintermediating legacy systems like SWIFT. Unresolved questions persist about interoperability between the expanding array of stablecoins and blockchains, the risks and frictions of dollarization, and the ongoing challenges for regulators and banks in adapting to this new landscape.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- The current crypto cycle in Africa is distinct because it’s driven by real utility from stablecoins, not speculation or non-fungible tokens.
- Yellowcard pivoted from retail crypto exchange to a B2B and institutional payments focus, in response to overwhelming demand for stablecoins over Bitcoin.
- Stablecoins are primarily used for cross-border business payments and global dollar access, not for everyday local transactions.
- Weaknesses or exclusions in traditional banking and SWIFT infrastructure in emerging markets create strong demand for alternatives like stablecoins.
- There are significant regional variations in adoption—with West Africa being the most aggressive in integrating new tech, Southern Africa focusing on regulation, and East Africa moving towards integrating stablecoin infrastructure with banks.
- Regulatory attitudes across Africa have generally shifted in favor of clear frameworks for stablecoins; countries now seek to benefit economically from being early adopters.
- Despite regulatory progress, concerns about dollarization and loss of monetary sovereignty remain, although the present impact is mostly limited to international, not domestic, payments.
- Tether (USDT), especially on Tron, remains the dominant stablecoin for payments, largely due to its early focus on global integration and liquidity in local exchanges, while USDC and others lag in emerging markets.
- A major technical and cost friction is the lack of seamless interoperability between multiple stablecoins and blockchains; market needs clearer, cheaper clearing infrastructure.
- Banks and incumbent financial institutions are beginning to recognize the necessity of integrating stablecoin infrastructure to remain relevant, as business clients are leading adoption out of sheer need.
- Future success depends on making stablecoin-powered payments invisible to end-users, integrating them fully within institutional and consumer banking systems, where users need not handle private keys or wallet addresses.
