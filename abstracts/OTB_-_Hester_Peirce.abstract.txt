PART 1 — ABSTRACT

This conversation centers on the SEC’s evolving approach to regulating blockchain and crypto assets, featuring insights from Commissioner Hester Peirce. It explores the SEC’s internal struggles, the boundaries of its jurisdiction, and the persistent absence of clear, innovation-friendly regulatory frameworks for digital assets in the U.S. The dialogue highlights the adverse impact of regulatory ambiguity—driving entrepreneurs and innovation abroad, as well as fostering bad behavior due to unclear rules—while considering the respective roles of Congress, the SEC, and industry in building effective oversight. The future remains uncertain regarding how and when meaningful clarity will emerge, what form disclosure and registration requirements should take for various assets, and how agencies will balance investor protection with fostering responsible innovation.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- Regulatory ambiguity and enforcement-first approaches by the SEC have stifled U.S. crypto innovation, causing talented entrepreneurs to avoid or leave the space and driving activity offshore.
- There is tension within the SEC itself, with differing commissioner perspectives on crypto and a lack of effective collaboration or proactive policy development in recent years.
- Regulatory missteps—such as broad interpretations of the Howey test and sweeping declarations that all tokens are securities—have muddied legal clarity and discouraged constructive experimentation.
- Overreliance on enforcement actions, instead of clear rulemaking or guidance, has drained resources and deterred legitimate actors while failing to adequately target bad actors.
- The lack of robust, transparent frameworks for token offerings, disclosures, and secondary markets has allowed fraud and abuses to persist while preventing mainstream institutional engagement.
- Legislative action is likely necessary to resolve current jurisdictional uncertainties (e.g., where crypto assets don’t fit existing categories or overlap SEC and CFTC mandates), but meaningful progress has been slow.
- Commissioner Peirce’s safe harbor proposals offer a possible interim model, but practical challenges remain, especially around transitions between security and non-security status and workable disclosure regimes for small teams.
- Industry self-regulation (standardized disclosures, proof-of-reserves audits) might productively complement formal regulation, but it requires regulator buy-in and clarity about legal liabilities.
- Tokenized securities and on-chain financial products are seen as promising, yet continued regulatory barriers around custody, trading, and market structure inhibit capital formation and venture investment.
- Both public and private sector education is necessary for regulators to keep pace with technological change, and open lines of communication between industry players and regulators are critical—but trust is fragile after years of adversarial encounters.
