# Why Central Banks Should Ditch Gold and Embrace Tokenized Finance

## Abstract

PART 1 — ABSTRACT

This conversation fundamentally debates the rationale for central banks continuing to hold gold, contending that gold's value is mostly a fragile social construct rather than grounded in intrinsic utility. Instead, the discussion advocates for central banks to shift towards holding diversified portfolios of real and financial assets, and to embrace developments in tokenized finance—particularly fully-backed stablecoins and future central bank digital currencies (CBDCs). The discussion situates this pivot within broader concerns about currency debasement, inflation, and systemic risk in global finance, arguing that programmable, transparent, and robust tokenized instruments could increase efficiency and stability. Unresolved tensions remain around the practical implementation of private versus public tokenized money, regulatory treatment, the privacy and control implications of CBDCs, and how to manage the transition from legacy to digital infrastructure without destabilizing financial intermediation or increasing inequality.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- Gold is described as a historically persistent but ultimately unstable "bubble," lacking meaningful intrinsic value and deriving its worth mainly from collective belief, making it a questionable reserve for central banks.
- Central banks are encouraged to sell their gold reserves and instead hold diversified portfolios of real assets and financial instruments to better protect taxpayers and reduce concentration risk.
- Bitcoin is viewed as riskier and less suitable for central bank balance sheets due to its volatility and lack of intrinsic backing, despite having more utility as a means of payment than gold.
- The argument is made that stablecoins, if fully backed and capable of paying interest, could technologically surpass traditional bank deposits and even rival central bank money, provided regulatory requirements enforce backing with reserves or short-term sovereign debt.
- Concerns about stablecoins disrupting bank deposits are dismissed, with the claim that the monetary system can adapt by allowing banks to intermediate via other mechanisms if needed.
- Tokenized deposits (fractionally-backed digital liabilities of commercial banks) are viewed as continuing the risks and regulatory challenges of current fractional reserve banking, while fully-backed stablecoins could obviate the need for deposit insurance and lender-of-last-resort facilities.
- CBDCs are positioned as potentially superior to paper currency—especially if widely accessible, interest-bearing, and protected by cryptographic privacy layers—though political resistance, particularly in the U.S., remains strong.
- Private stablecoins and CBDCs are seen as likely competitors, but progress and innovation (such as faster and more reliable payments) are expected to emanate from the private sector even if central banks offer digital currencies.
- Institutional access to central bank accounts (for stablecoin issuers or corporations) is framed as advantageous, potentially leveling the playing field between private and public digital money issuance.
- The looming proliferation of private stablecoins, including those from banks, tech companies, and other consortia, may be limited by high setup and operational costs, with the ultimate winners depending on backing, regulation, and ability to scale cross-border.
