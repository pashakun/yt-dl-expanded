# Why Stablecoins Are The Banks’ Worst Nightmare

## Abstract

PART 1 — ABSTRACT

This conversation examines how stablecoins threaten to fundamentally disrupt the traditional banking sector by severing the deep political and economic ties between banks and nation-states, which have historically relied on chartered institutions to control and bundle payment and lending functions. The participants argue that stablecoins are not just a technological innovation but deeply political instruments whose success depends on how well their proponents maneuver existing coalitions of power, regulatory frameworks, and incumbent interests like the Federal Reserve and large banks. The discussion matters because it highlights the potential for stablecoins, if embedded in appropriate legal and regulatory frameworks, to decouple payment systems from banks, undermine existing privileges, and possibly transform the very definition and operation of money, units of account, and even monetary policy. However, significant questions remain about political resistance, future regulatory adaptations (such as constraints on stablecoin yield or reserve assets), the risk of recapture by incumbent interests, and whether decentralized innovations can truly realize their promise without being co-opted or restricted by entrenched powers.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- Banks are not simply financial intermediaries but politically chartered entities whose structures, privileges, and bailouts are outcomes of bargains among powerful interest groups within the nation-state.
- Stablecoins, despite being technologically mature, have been held back primarily by political resistance from incumbent banks and their allies, not by technical or economic barriers.
- Recent shifts (e.g., the Genius Act) reflect cracks in the political coalition against stablecoin adoption, but legal and political battles over stablecoin design, regulation, and privileges are ongoing and unresolved.
- The unbundling of payments and lending via stablecoins threatens the core bundling model that sustains bank profitability and political influence, potentially removing banks’ privileged control over both deposits and payment rails.
- Political and regulatory frameworks (not just markets or technology) will ultimately determine whether stablecoins can compete fairly, receive charters, and offer consumer benefits like yield.
- There is acute concern that incumbent interests will seek to limit stablecoin value (e.g., via yield restrictions) or re-bundle these innovations into quasi-bank structures, neutralizing competitive threats.
- Stablecoins’ rise could strengthen the dollar’s international role in the short run by extending dollar-denominated mediums of exchange to global users, but may eventually enable new, more optimal units of account based on consumption bundles, potentially redefining the currency landscape.
- The transition to blockchain-based payment systems could allow for greater monetary policy flexibility (e.g., via fiscal tools rather than central bank control) and democratized innovation, but risks around inflation, “fiscal dominance,” and loss of government control remain.
- National responses will diverge: small, homogeneous countries may embrace stablecoins and benefit from openness, while major incumbents (U.S., EU) are prone to protect existing banking coalitions, using regulation as a political tool.
- Achieving the economic and social value of stablecoins requires ongoing, organized political advocacy, as entrenched interests will continuously adapt and seek to recapture or stifle disruptive innovations.
