## Abstract

PART 1 — ABSTRACT

The conversation centers on the evolving landscape of stablecoins and their increasingly pivotal role in the strategies of both crypto-native and traditional financial institutions. With widespread institutional interest and a rush of new entrants—including established banks, payment processors, fintechs, and decentralized protocols—the debate focuses on the mechanisms, tradeoffs, and trajectories for the next phase of stablecoin adoption and infrastructure. Why this matters is that stablecoins have become the “product–market fit” for crypto, and their design, regulation, and distribution channels have broad implications for cross-border payments, financial disintermediation, and the competitive positioning of legacy institutions. Unresolved questions include how distribution networks will ultimately determine winners, whether regulatory clarity will keep pace with product experimentation, and how far incumbent players can evolve or defend their business models as composable, yield-generating, and programmable dollar analogs become commonplace.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- Distribution remains the primary bottleneck and differentiator; regardless of technical design, stablecoins only achieve scale if embedded within large user ecosystems (e.g., payment processors, exchanges, fintech apps).
- There is increasing convergence between crypto-native entities (Athena, Tempo, Plasma) and traditional institutions (Visa, Mastercard, JPMorgan, Western Union), with incumbents now actively building or supporting their own stablecoin solutions.
- Regulatory developments (e.g., the GENIUS Act in the US), including debates over permissible yield and types of stablecoins (payment vs. savings), will shape both product architectures and the business models available to issuers.
- Stablecoin-as-a-service (white-label issuance) is seeing broad experimentation, but ecosystem and incentive alignment challenges—especially around liquidity provision and competition between partners—may limit its pace of adoption outside certain use cases.
- Incumbents are threatened by transparent, yield-bearing stablecoins, as these challenge the bank model of profiting from low-access retail deposits; thus, banks must either adapt or risk disintermediation.
- Demand for stablecoins, especially for international users and cross-border settlements, far exceeds current permissioned lending and yield infrastructure on-chain, creating persistent demand, capital rotation, and sometimes misaligned incentives.
- Technical design choices (e.g., whether stablecoin-focused chains or layer-2s should be their own L1s or tied to major blockchains like Ethereum) reflect tradeoffs between interoperability, control, censorship resistance, and ease of integration with financial primitives.
- Liquidity bootstrapping for new stablecoins—especially for those aiming at compliance and off-chain backing—remains cost-intensive, driving the importance of native, composable on-chain liquidity pools and market-maker incentives.
- Institutional entry is accelerating, not only via investments and acquisitions (e.g., MasterCard’s pursuit of ZeroHash) but also through direct product launches and integrations with blockchain infrastructure, signaling stablecoins’ mainstreaming trajectory.
- The ultimate winners may be those able to both subsidize early adoption (potentially via token incentives) and navigate regulatory ambiguity, while offering seamless interoperability between institutional and retail use cases.
