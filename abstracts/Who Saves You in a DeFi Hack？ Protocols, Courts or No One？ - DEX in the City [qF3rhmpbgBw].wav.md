## Abstract

PART 1 — ABSTRACT  
This discussion centers on the complex question of who is responsible for protecting users in the event of a DeFi (decentralized finance) hack or exploit, and what forms of recourse are available—if any—when things go wrong. The hosts, drawing on their legal and industry experience, analyze the blurry line between decentralization and the need for user safety, exploring whether solutions should be technological, community-driven, or imposed through regulation or courts. This debate is fundamental to the evolution and mainstream adoption of crypto and DeFi, as confidence and institutional participation depend on clear standards and accountability. Yet, tensions persist around how much risk users should assume, whether best practices should become enforceable norms, and how the regulatory landscape, notably stalled U.S. legislation, will ultimately resolve these questions.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- Decentralization in DeFi is not absolute; in practice, technical and social interventions blur the ideal of "code is law."
- Hack responses differ widely: some chains and protocols intervene to mitigate losses, while others adhere strictly to immutability, creating uncertainty for users.
- There is an inherent tradeoff between user freedom and user protections; both extremes—radical decentralization and heavy regulation—carry risks for adoption.
- Technical and procedural safeguards (like smart contract audits and risk management tools) are becoming informal industry standards despite the absence of mandates.
- The ability for users and institutions to choose their desired level of security or recourse could drive DeFi’s growth, but makes universal standards elusive.
- Legal recourse through courts is unpredictable and often inadequate for resolving sophisticated exploits, as shown by recent high-profile hung juries and overturned verdicts.
- Institutional adoption and market trust hinge on users knowing in advance what protections and recourse exist, which remains inconsistent in DeFi.
- Regulatory approaches are in flux: ongoing legislative efforts in the U.S. (notably market structure bills) have left core issues—like DeFi oversight—unresolved, creating further uncertainty.
- There is a risk that premature or blunt regulation in response to security concerns could stifle innovation or reintroduce unwanted centralization.
- Competitive advantage may emerge for protocols that proactively embed safety and recourse features, influencing the design and user composition of future DeFi platforms.
