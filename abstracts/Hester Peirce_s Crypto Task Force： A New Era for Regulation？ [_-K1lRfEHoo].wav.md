## Abstract

PART 1 — ABSTRACT

The conversation centers on changing regulatory approaches to crypto under new leadership at the SEC, specifically through the establishment of a dedicated Crypto Task Force led by Commissioner Hester Peirce. This matters because the previous era was characterized by regulatory uncertainty, aggressive enforcement, and a lack of clarity—dampening innovation and straining relations with both crypto projects and traditional institutions. The discussion raises hopes for more open engagement, clearer rules, and temporary relief mechanisms for past token offerings, while seeking to balance investor protection and the need for experimentation. Key tensions remain unresolved: how different tokens (such as meme coins and airdrops) are classified, how interagency boundaries (SEC vs CFTC) are drawn, and whether broader structural barriers—like investor accreditation—will truly be addressed or simply reformed around the edges.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- The repeal of SAB 121 is intended to allow more entities, including traditional custodians, to safely participate in crypto custody without prohibitive costs, potentially increasing institutional involvement.
- The new Crypto Task Force aims to provide more transparent, predictable, and consultative regulation, contrasting with past antagonistic or opaque approaches.
- There is discussion of temporary "safe harbor" or retroactive relief for past token offerings, with disclosure requirements, to resolve regulatory uncertainty and encourage good-faith compliance.
- No-action letters and broad guidance may offer alternative routes for projects to determine whether or not they fall within SEC jurisdiction—but these mechanisms depend on facts and circumstances, preserving some ambiguity.
- Interagency friction between the SEC and CFTC over the classification of assets as securities or commodities is acknowledged; there is an expressed intent to move toward more collaborative and rational boundary-setting.
- The conversation questions if open, permissionless networks can genuinely operate under securities law without undermining core crypto features (like peer-to-peer transfer and pseudonymity), exposing a major regulatory design challenge.
- Tensions remain between investor protection, market access, and the democratic ideal of financial participation—exemplified by the debate over accredited investor rules and new ideas for retail access to private markets.
- The SEC recognizes that not all digital assets, especially meme coins or certain airdrops, automatically fall under securities law, but stresses that anti-fraud statutes still apply—limiting the scope for total laissez-faire activity.
- Market participants remain wary of engagement due to prior negative experiences with the SEC; rebuilding trust is seen as a process requiring demonstrable regulatory goodwill and real-world successes.
- Remaining frictions: outstanding enforcement actions, lack of clarity for DeFi and custody, challenges for US-based airdrops, and unresolved questions about future ETFs and product approvals.
