## Abstract

PART 1 — ABSTRACT  
This discussion centers on why most blockchain tokenization projects in finance fail to achieve impact, and why Figure’s approach to tokenizing home equity lines of credit (HELOCs) succeeded where others faltered. The conversation explores the practical challenges and institutional frictions that persist in moving real-world assets (RWAs) on-chain, especially the difficulty in creating liquid secondary markets and in aligning blockchain’s benefits with entrenched financial practices. The conversation matters because it highlights the gradual but significant way blockchain might reshape core activities like lending and securitization, with implications for costs, transparency, and market access. Yet, uncertainty remains over the willingness of large banks to adopt shared standards, how regulation will define and protect DeFi, and whether the ecosystem can endure the short-term inefficiencies necessary to unlock long-term transformation.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- Most tokenization projects fail because switching to blockchain introduces initial friction and cost, rather than immediate efficiency or liquidity; successful projects require long-term commitment and willingness to “leave money on the table” to reach scale.
- Figure’s success in tokenizing HELOCs comes from targeting a segment (sub-$300k first-lien mortgages) that was unprofitable using legacy rails, making blockchain-native origination and processing a genuine competitive advantage.
- Blockchain’s immutability lowers operational and compliance costs, such as reducing audit requirements in securitization, which generates tangible, ongoing savings.
- Creating liquidity for tokenized assets is non-trivial; persistent liquidity emerges only with homogeneous asset pools, standardized agreements, and real-time performance data—counterexamples include tokenized office buildings or private stock, which do not trade frequently.
- DeFi involvement enables more direct and potentially cheaper funding for tokenized assets (e.g., Figure moving warehouse funding onto DeFi platforms), but requires assets to be liquid and introduces tensions around KYC/AML and decentralization.
- The adoption of stablecoins and blockchain settlement in institutional contexts is hampered by the lack of real-world utility and regulatory clarity, though incoming legislation (such as the GENIUS Act) may catalyze broader usage and competition among banks.
- Large incumbent banks (e.g., JPMorgan) are emerging as key innovators, threatening regional banks by deploying proprietary stablecoins and rails that could disintermediate parts of today’s payments and deposit systems.
- Regulatory uncertainty remains pronounced, especially over what counts as “true” DeFi versus centralized control in protocols, how to distinguish between decentralized governance and disguised intermediaries, and how legislation will ultimately treat peer-to-peer on-chain transactions.
- There is philosophical tension between building centralized “super apps” in finance versus enabling user-controlled, self-custodied wallets that access decentralized applications—the latter may enable more user choice but require significant wallet UX improvements.
- The ultimate vision is for blockchain-native origination, settlement, and trading of both credit and equity securities, displacing legacy infrastructure (like DTC/NSCC), but realization depends on continued institutional buy-in and regulatory alignment.
