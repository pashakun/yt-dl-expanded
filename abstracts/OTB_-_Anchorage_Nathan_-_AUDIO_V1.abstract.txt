PART 1 — ABSTRACT

This conversation centers on the interplay between crypto industry access to financial infrastructure, evolving regulatory frameworks, and the institutionalization of digital assets, as seen through the lens of Anchorage’s experience as a federally chartered digital asset bank. The discussion highlights the recent wave of “debanking” affecting crypto companies, the policy uncertainty surrounding digital asset custody, and the prospects for forthcoming regulation—such as stablecoin legislation and broader crypto market structure reform—that could shape the sector’s future. The decisions of traditional financial institutions about whether to build, buy, or partner for crypto custody services, and the challenges of creating scalable, fair stablecoins are explored as pivotal issues for industry maturation. The conversation underscores that while regulatory momentum appears to be growing, crucial questions about the balance between regulatory clarity, competition, and fair access for innovators remain unresolved, as do uncertainties over how legislative or interpretive developments will ultimately reshape market structure and institutional participation.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- The federal bank charter obtained by Anchorage marked a regulatory milestone, allowing direct institutional participation in the digital asset space and signaling tacit approval from regulators, though access to such charters has since closed.
- Regulatory uncertainty and targeted regulatory actions, exemplified by SAB 121 and interpretive letters, created a chilling effect, causing both traditional banks and crypto firms to retreat from partnerships and, in many cases, experience debanking.
- There is emerging bipartisan recognition in Washington that debanking—whether of crypto firms or other politically disfavored groups—is problematic and potentially unacceptable as a matter of policy.
- SAB 121, by imposing strict accounting treatments, effectively deterred banks from entering crypto custody, temporarily creating near-monopoly conditions for existing players but raising concerns about fairness and competition.
- The build-versus-buy-versus-partner dilemma for traditional financial institutions entering crypto custody is complicated by technical, regulatory, and talent constraints, pushing most toward sub-custodian partnerships with firms like Anchorage.
- Prospective stablecoin legislation is expected to arrive before broader market structure reform, with a growing consensus in policy circles that stablecoins represent a strategic asset for U.S. dollar influence; consortium-based models are being explored to address fairness and scalability.
- Current stablecoin economics often concentrate value capture among a few entities (e.g., USDC), prompting calls for new models—like the “global dollar network”—that share economics among a broader set of participants and enable true institutional scale.
- Regulatory vagueness around tokenized securities has constrained institutional experimentation and product development, with the hope that upcoming market structure legislation or agency rulemaking will unlock further innovation.
- New Anchorage products—such as Porto (self-custody wallet for institutional on-chain interaction) and Atlas (settlement infrastructure facilitating secure, atomic swaps and collateralized lending via tri-party relationships)—are designed to address gaps in market infrastructure brought on by legacy service shortfalls (like the loss of SEN/SigNet) and to lower institutional barriers to entry.
- The broad trajectory of debate and development in the sector hinges on whether regulatory agencies and/or Congress will provide enough clarity and coordination for institutions to confidently expand digital asset offerings, and how competitive dynamics will play out in an environment transitioning from regulatory exclusion to regulatory endorsement.
