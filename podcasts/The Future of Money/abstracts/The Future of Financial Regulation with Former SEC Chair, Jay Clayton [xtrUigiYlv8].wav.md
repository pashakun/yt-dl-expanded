## Abstract

PART 1 — ABSTRACT

This conversation centers on the evolving landscape of financial regulation in the face of technological disruption, particularly through the lens of crypto assets and blockchain-based innovations. Jay Clayton, the former SEC chair, reflects on the complexities of regulating new financial technologies within frameworks built for older paradigms, highlighting both the resistance of existing rules to fundamental change and the costly consequences of lagging regulatory expertise. The discussion underscores critical frictions between incumbent regulatory approaches—characterized by slow adoption and ambiguity—and the crypto sector's desire for clarity and incremental pathways for integration. Unresolved tensions include the inherent ambiguities in asset classification, the slow pace of policy adaptation, questions around whether specialized or harmonized regulatory models will prevail internationally, and the challenge of updating regulators’ skillsets to keep pace with innovation.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- Regulatory frameworks, especially in the US, are slow to adapt to technological advances such as crypto, partly due to outdated skillsets and a lack of technologists in government roles.
- Effective regulation has historically been enforced by maintaining strict standards around capital raising (e.g., public ICOs) and trading, creating significant friction for technology-enabled entrants who view these distinctions as obsolete.
- There is lingering ambiguity and frustration in the industry regarding the classification of crypto assets (security versus non-security), but former regulators argue the current principles are clearer than commonly claimed.
- The crypto sector erred in initially believing that foundational financial regulations would materially bend for new technology, and in pushing for sweeping legislative overhauls instead of pursuing incremental, attainable changes—such as stablecoin regulation and custody solutions.
- Regulation by enforcement—rather than proactive rulemaking—remains a contentious practice, with critics seeing it as stifling and proponents viewing it as necessary given unchanged statutory mandates.
- The lack of clear regulatory pathways for exchanges wishing to register for trading digital securities is seen as a major barrier, but the greater issue may be the slow willingness of regulators to adapt frameworks to accommodate new, more efficient technologies.
- There is recognition that technological integration (AI, blockchain, etc.) could and should collapse traditional regulatory silos across securities, commodities, and payments, but this will require new regulatory mindsets and talent.
- Internationally, some jurisdictions (e.g., Dubai/Abu Dhabi) are experimenting with more agile, tech-friendly regulatory frameworks—including specialized crypto regulators—potentially outpacing the US in innovation-readiness.
- The eventual approval of a Bitcoin ETF is seen as inevitable, signaling regulatory acceptance of digital asset custody and trading infrastructure, though the slow timeline is attributed to both genuine market risks and institutional readiness issues.
- Underlying political and perception challenges (e.g., persistent association of crypto with illicit activity) complicate legislative and regulatory progress, compounded by the limited bandwidth and risk aversion of both regulators and lawmakers.
