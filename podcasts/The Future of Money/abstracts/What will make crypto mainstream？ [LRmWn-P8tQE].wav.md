# What will make crypto mainstream?

## Abstract

PART 1 — ABSTRACT

This discussion critically explores what needs to happen for crypto adoption to reach mainstream, billion-user scale, focusing on technological, regulatory, and product-driven catalysts. The panel weighs the merits and limitations of tokenization, DeFi, user experience, institutional and banking engagement, regulatory clarity, and the role of stablecoins, while emphasizing both the opportunities and persistent frictions. There is consensus that infrastructural innovation and practical, user-friendly applications—often via abstracted or hybrid Web 2.5 models—are pivotal, but debates remain around whether centralized or decentralized approaches best align with crypto’s foundational values. Key unresolved tensions involve user experience versus self-sovereignty, the impact of financialization and speculation, the role of legacy and crypto-native institutions, and whether regulatory or market-driven factors will ultimately unlock mass adoption. The conversation underlines that while momentum is building, trade-offs around security, utility, and inclusivity—along with persistent counterparty risks and regulatory uncertainty—continue to shape crypto’s pathway to the mainstream.

PART 2 — KEY IDEAS

- Tokenization of real-world assets is seen as a major driver of mainstream adoption due to efficiency gains, institutional involvement, and expanding use cases, though the time horizon for impact remains uncertain.
- DeFi’s core value remains hindered by poor usability and complexity, limiting its accessibility to non-technical users; real adoption may require abstracted interfaces or hybrid solutions, but excessive focus on speculation crowds out substantive financial innovation.
- Institutional engagement, particularly from asset managers and new “crypto-native” banks or neo-banks, is accelerating, but legacy financial institutions are seen as slow to adapt, with new entrants likely to bridge the gap for everyday users.
- Stablecoins are experiencing rapid, organic growth and are identified as a practical “killer app,” especially for payments and settlements, though high access costs in emerging markets and dollar dominance present challenges.
- Improved custody and security solutions are necessary but not sufficient, acting as adoption gatekeepers rather than direct adoption drivers; ongoing hacks and scams damage trust and uptake.
- The notion of “Web 2.5”—where familiar Web 2 products abstract away crypto’s complexity while delivering Web 3 benefits—is viewed by some as critical to onboarding mainstream users, but others warn it can erode the ethos of decentralization if not designed with genuine self-custody and censorship resistance.
- Regulatory clarity is near-universally recognized as a prerequisite for mainstream adoption and institutional investment; lack of it, particularly in the US, is holding the industry back, while friendly jurisdictions are seeing faster growth.
- CBDCs are broadly dismissed as negative or counterproductive to crypto adoption, mainly due to privacy concerns and unresolved structural questions, though regional differences may yield divergent outcomes.
- The presence and quality of venture capital in crypto are divisive: fast, speculative, and misaligned VC capital can fragment talent and incentivize short-termism, undermining ecosystem development.
- The largest risks identified are counterparty failures, dominance of gambling-like speculative activity, and insufficient user-friendly solutions, all of which hamper sustainable, broad-based crypto adoption.
